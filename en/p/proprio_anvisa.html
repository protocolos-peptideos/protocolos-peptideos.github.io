Experimental material. Read before using anything from here.

Nothing on this page is a medical recommendation, prescription or treatment plan. It is an organized translation of protocols circulating in research communities and, where it exists, of what published trials tested. The two are marked differently — and they are not equivalent.

Most of the compounds here have no ANVISA or FDA approval for human use. Several are sold labeled "research use only", which means they have not gone through purity, sterility or dosage controls for human consumption. A community-reported dose is not a validated dose: it is what someone reported having done.

Talk to a licensed health professional before considering any of these compounds. If you already use one and feel anything unexpected, seek care — do not wait for the next routine test.

Summary

A sweep of ANVISA's official open data — 43,489 drug records, downloaded on September 4, 2026 — for every compound on the site, by active ingredient and by product name. Four exist: semaglutide, tirzepatide, oxytocin and methylene blue. The other forty have no Brazilian package insert, no approved dose and no inspected batch. The result now appears on every compound page, in its own line. The most unexpected side finding: generics and similars of semaglutide are already registered in Brazil, from EMS, Germed, Sun and others — fifteen products in all.

This page's question

The other primary-source pages ask how much evidence exists and is the dose right. This one asks the simplest of the three, and the one nobody answers: does this legally exist in Brazil?

The GLP-1 audit was only possible because there was a package insert. On finishing it, the obvious doubt remained: how many of the compounds on this site have a package insert? I went to count. I downloaded ANVISA's official open data — 43,489 drug records — and searched for each compound by active ingredient and by product name.

The result is now on every compound page, in a line right below the general notice. This page is the consolidated result and the method.

The number

Of the 44 protocol compounds on this site, 40 have no registered drug in Brazil.

It is not that registration is hard, or that ANVISA is slow. It is that almost none of this was even submitted. There is no Brazilian package insert, no approved dose, no inspected batch and no one to complain to. What circulates is personal importation or compounding.

Registration and notification are not the same thing

The database has two statuses that look alike and are not. Registration goes through analysis of efficacy, safety and quality, and receives a number. Notification is the low-risk route: the manufacturer reports, and the product enters without the same scrutiny — in the database it appears without a registration number.

This took work, and nearly ruined the count. Since notified products have no number, a first version of this sweep grouped them all into a single line and undercounted methylene blue. I corrected it by separating the two columns. Where the page says notified, that is what it means.

How to check what is here

The whole method fits in three steps, and anyone can repeat it:

StepWhat I did
1. SourceI downloaded DADOS_ABERTOS_MEDICAMENTOS.csv from ANVISA's open data portal on September 4, 2026. It has 43,489 rows, with active ingredient, product name, regulatory category, company and registration status
2. SearchFor each compound, I searched for the term in the active ingredient and in the product name, ignoring accents and case, counting only entries with status Active. Inactive registrations appear in the database and were discarded
3. ControlThis is the part that validates the rest. Before believing any zero, I tested the method with things that necessarily exist: insulin, dipyrone and methylene blue. All three came back positive. And I included an entire control group — the prescription-only items —, which came back 4 out of 5

The wrong path, worth recording

The first attempt was through the electronic package insert database, which is the obvious query. It returned zero for forty compounds, and I almost published that.

What saved it was the control: I ran insulin through the package-insert database and it also returned zero. Insulin, obviously, is registered in Brazil. The package-insert database searches by product name, and insulin is sold as Lantus, Humalog, Tresiba — the substance name matches nothing.

Forty correct zeros for the wrong reason are still an invalid result. It was only by switching to the open data, which carries the active ingredient in its own column, that the zeros started to mean something.

What 'not registered' means, and what it does not

  • It means: there is no drug with that active ingredient approved for sale in Brazil. No Brazilian package insert, no approved dosing, no batch inspection and no legally responsible party.
  • It does not mean the substance is illegal to possess. Personal importation in small quantities has its own rules, and is not the subject of this page.
  • It does not mean it does not exist in another category. The open data used here lists drugs. Dietary supplements, cosmetics and medical devices have separate databases and were left out — that is why creatine and vitamin D do not appear, although they are legal and common.
  • It does not mean it does not work. Registration is a regulatory decision, not a scientific verdict. This site's evidence pages answer a different question, and sometimes disagree with this one.

What this sweep did not do

  • I did not check the package inserts of those with registration, except for the four GLP-1s from the previous audit. Knowing that a registration exists is not knowing what the package insert says.
  • I did not search chemical synonyms exhaustively. I used the current name, the known brand name and the Portuguese denomination. If any compound is registered under a synonym I did not test, it appears here as zero — and that would be my error.
  • I did not check the supplement and medical device databases, which are separate.
  • I did not look at import status or the applicable RDC. The page answers whether a registration exists, and nothing else.
